\subsubsection{Physical activity}

Daily step counts were recorded with a smartphone (Redmi Note 11S/Pedometer Step Counter) 
from January 2023, and with a wrist-worn watch (Dyurawa) from March 2025. The phone records only the
steps taken while it is carried, so it misses most activity at home, whereas the watch
records activity throughout the day; watch counts were therefore taken as the reference
measure.

Gaps of up to four consecutive days were filled by linear interpolation in both series, if missing
days were less than 10\%. Days on which the watch recorded fewer steps than the phone were considered days of
watch non-wear or malfunction, and their watch counts were set to missing.

To obtain a continuous series of watch-equivalent steps over the whole study period
(\texttt{Steps.C}), watch counts were estimated from phone counts on days without watch
data. The relationship between the two counts differs between days spent at home, when
the phone is mostly not carried and its count carries little information, and days with
outdoor activity, when the phone is carried. Days were therefore divided into two regimes
by a threshold $t$ on phone steps (\texttt{Steps}), and a linear regression of watch steps 
(\texttt{Steps.B}) on phone steps was fitted separately in each regime. The threshold was chosen by scanning values from
50 to 2000 phone steps and selecting the one that minimised the total sum of squared
residuals of the two regressions, requiring at least 20 days in each regime
(see Figure~\ref{fig:steps-model}). On days with watch data,
\texttt{Steps.C} equals the measured watch count; on the other days, it is the value
predicted by the regression of the corresponding regime.

The imputation was validated by fitting the model on the first 80\% of the watch period
and comparing the values predicted for the remaining 20\% with the measured ones (Figure~\ref{fig:steps-validation}). 
The relative mean absolute error, $\sum|\hat{y}_i - y_i| / \sum y_i$, and the relative mean error
(bias), $\sum(\hat{y}_i - y_i) / \sum y_i$, are indicated on the plot. Most days were slightly overestimated,
while the most active days, and outdoor activity without the phone, were underestimated. The time series 
of \texttt{Steps}, \texttt{Steps.B}, and \texttt{Steps.C} are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:steps}.

\subsubsection{Heart rate variability}

Heart rate variability (HRV) was assessed from five-minute recordings of RR intervals,
following the protocol and the analysis pipeline described in detail in a previous
work \citep{maccallini2024hrv}, to which the reader is referred for an overview on
HRV, the definition of the indices, their physiological interpretation, and an introduction to
spectral analysis by Discrete Fourier Transform. Recordings started in July 2022 and 
were performed once a day, in the morning, in the supine position, after five minutes of rest in the same position.
RR intervals were acquired for about five minutes with a Polar H9 sensor mounted on a
moistened chest belt, a type of device whose agreement with the electrocardiogram has
been documented \citep{bourdillon2017}. The sensor was connected via Bluetooth to a
smartphone running the Elite HRV application, and the RR series were exported in text
format, one file per recording.

The series were analysed with the R package RHRV \citep{garcia2017rhrv}. For each
recording, the instantaneous non-interpolated heart rate was computed from the RR
intervals, and anomalous beats (ectopic beats and artefacts) were removed with the
adaptive filter of the package, which discards beats that differ too much from the
preceding ones or that fall outside physiological limits (default parameters). The
filtered series was then linearly interpolated at \SI{4}{\hertz} to obtain the evenly
sampled signal required by the discrete Fourier transform. Recordings shorter than
\SI{240}{\second} or longer than \SI{360}{\second} were excluded, and when more than
one recording was available for the same day only the first one was retained.

In the time domain, the following indices were computed: mean heart rate (HR) and
mean RR interval (RR); the standard deviation of the RR intervals (SDNN) and the HRV
triangular index (HRVi), as measures of overall variability; the root mean square of
successive differences (rMSSD) and the percentage of successive differences greater
than \SI{50}{\milli\second} (pNN50), as measures of the high-frequency, vagally
mediated component \citep{garcia2017rhrv,shaffer2017}.

In the frequency domain, the power spectrum was estimated with the short-time Fourier
transform, using a single window of \SI{300}{\second}, since
for five-minute recordings shorter windows give less reliable estimates of the
low-frequency power \citep{bourdillon2017}. The power was integrated over the
low-frequency (LF, \SIrange{0.04}{0.15}{\hertz}) and the high-frequency (HF,
\SIrange{0.15}{0.40}{\hertz}) bands \citep{taskforce1996}. The ultra-low- and
very-low-frequency bands were not considered, since they cannot be reliably estimated
from five-minute recordings \citep{taskforce1996}. From LF and HF, the following derived
quantities were computed: the ratio LF/HF; the total power, defined here as
$\mathrm{TotPower} = \mathrm{LF} + \mathrm{HF}$; and the normalised powers
$\mathrm{LF_{nu}} = \mathrm{LF}/(\mathrm{LF}+\mathrm{HF})$ and
$\mathrm{HF_{nu}} = \mathrm{HF}/(\mathrm{LF}+\mathrm{HF})$, which reduce the within- and
between-subject variability of the absolute powers \citep{burr2007}. The distributions
of the time-domain and frequency-domain indices over all the recordings are shown in
Figures~\ref{fig:hrv-time} and~\ref{fig:hrv-freq}.
