\subsubsection{Physical activity}

Daily step counts were recorded with a smartphone (Redmi Note 11S/Pedometer Step Counter) 
from January 2023, and with a wrist-worn watch (Dyurawa) from March 2025. The phone records only the
steps taken while it is carried, so it misses most activity at home, whereas the watch
records activity throughout the day; watch counts were therefore taken as the reference
measure.

Gaps of up to four consecutive days were filled by linear interpolation in both series, if missing
days were less than 10\%. Days on which the watch recorded fewer steps than the phone were considered days of
watch non-wear or malfunction, and their watch counts were set to missing.

To obtain a continuous series of watch-equivalent steps over the whole study period
(\texttt{Steps.C}), watch counts were estimated from phone counts on days without watch
data. The relationship between the two counts differs between days spent at home, when
the phone is mostly not carried and its count carries little information, and days with
outdoor activity, when the phone is carried. Days were therefore divided into two regimes
by a threshold $t$ on phone steps (\texttt{Steps}), and a linear regression of watch steps 
(\texttt{Steps.B}) on phone steps was fitted separately in each regime. The threshold was chosen by scanning values from
50 to 2000 phone steps and selecting the one that minimised the total sum of squared
residuals of the two regressions, requiring at least 20 days in each regime
(see Figure~\ref{fig:steps-model}). On days with watch data,
\texttt{Steps.C} equals the measured watch count; on the other days, it is the value
predicted by the regression of the corresponding regime.

The imputation was validated by fitting the model on the first 80\% of the watch period
and comparing the values predicted for the remaining 20\% with the measured ones (Figure~\ref{fig:steps-validation}). 
The relative mean absolute error, $\sum|\hat{y}_i - y_i| / \sum y_i$, and the relative mean error
(bias), $\sum(\hat{y}_i - y_i) / \sum y_i$, are indicated on the plot. Most days were slightly overestimated,
while the most active days, and outdoor activity without the phone, were underestimated. The time series 
of \texttt{Steps}, \texttt{Steps.B}, and \texttt{Steps.C} are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:steps-time-series}.

\subsubsection{Heart rate variability}

Heart rate variability (HRV) was assessed from five-minute recordings of RR intervals,
following the protocol and the analysis pipeline described in detail in a previous
work \citep{maccallini2024hrv}, to which the reader is referred for an overview on
HRV, the definition of the indices, their physiological interpretation, and an introduction to
spectral analysis by Discrete Fourier Transform. Recordings started on \hrvStart{} and, 
up to \hrvEnd{}, were available for \hrvDays{} days, and were performed once a day, 
simultaneously with the EEG and fNIRS recordings. They were performed once a day, in the morning, in the supine position, after five minutes of rest in the same position.
RR intervals were acquired for about five minutes with a Polar H9 sensor mounted on a
moistened chest belt, a type of device whose agreement with the electrocardiogram has
been documented \citep{bourdillon2017}. The sensor was connected via Bluetooth to a
smartphone running the Elite HRV application, and the RR series were exported in text
format, one file per recording. 

The series were analysed with the R package RHRV \citep{garcia2017rhrv}. For each
recording, the instantaneous non-interpolated heart rate was computed from the RR
intervals, and anomalous beats (ectopic beats and artefacts) were removed with the
adaptive filter of the package, which discards beats that differ too much from the
preceding ones or that fall outside physiological limits (default parameters). The
filtered series was then linearly interpolated at \SI{4}{\hertz} to obtain the evenly
sampled signal required by the discrete Fourier transform. Recordings shorter than
\SI{240}{\second} or longer than \SI{360}{\second} were excluded, and when more than
one recording was available for the same day only the first one was retained.

In the time domain, the following indices were computed: mean heart rate (HR) and
mean RR interval (RR); the standard deviation of the RR intervals (SDNN) and the HRV
triangular index (HRVi), as measures of overall variability; the root mean square of
successive differences (rMSSD) and the percentage of successive differences greater
than \SI{50}{\milli\second} (pNN50), as measures of the high-frequency, vagally
mediated component \citep{garcia2017rhrv,shaffer2017}.

In the frequency domain, the power spectrum was estimated with the short-time Fourier
transform, using a single window of \SI{300}{\second}, since
for five-minute recordings shorter windows give less reliable estimates of the
low-frequency power \citep{bourdillon2017}. The power was integrated over the
low-frequency (LF, \SIrange{0.04}{0.15}{\hertz}) and the high-frequency (HF,
\SIrange{0.15}{0.40}{\hertz}) bands \citep{taskforce1996}. The ultra-low- and
very-low-frequency bands were not considered, since they cannot be reliably estimated
from five-minute recordings \citep{taskforce1996}. From LF and HF, the following derived
quantities were computed: the ratio LF/HF; the total power, defined here as
$\mathrm{TotPower} = \mathrm{LF} + \mathrm{HF}$; and the normalised powers
$\mathrm{LF_{nu}} = \mathrm{LF}/(\mathrm{LF}+\mathrm{HF})$ and
$\mathrm{HF_{nu}} = \mathrm{HF}/(\mathrm{LF}+\mathrm{HF})$, which reduce the within- and
between-subject variability of the absolute powers \citep{burr2007}. The distributions
of the time-domain and frequency-domain indices over all the recordings are shown in
Figures~\ref{fig:hrv-time} and~\ref{fig:hrv-freq}.

\subsubsection{Blood pressure and lean test}

Blood pressure and heart rate were measured with an automatic upper-arm oscillometric
monitor (Omron M6 Comfort) immediately after the HRV recording
(Section~\ref{sec:hrv}), with the subject still in the supine position, that is after
at least ten minutes of supine rest. This measurement provided the supine values of
Systolic (SBP) and Diastolic (DBP) blood pressure and of heart rate (HR), and was
available on most days of the study.

On some days, the supine measurement was followed by a NASA lean test, performed
according to the protocol of the Bateman Horne Center \citep{bhc_nasa_lean}, a
variant of the passive standing test used by NASA to assess orthostatic intolerance
\citep{bungo1985}. The subject stood up and leaned against a wall with only the
shoulder blades in contact with it and the heels about \SI{15}{\centi\metre} from the
wall, keeping still and without talking, a position that reduces the contribution of
the leg muscle pump to venous return. Blood pressure and heart rate were then measured
during standing, and each measurement was assigned to the minute of standing in which
it was taken ($t = 1, 2, \dots, 10$ min). Unlike the original protocol, which
prescribes ten minutes of standing with measurements every minute, the test was
performed occasionally, its duration varied between 4 and 10 minutes, and not all
time points were recorded in every test; consequently, the later time points are
available for fewer tests than the earlier ones. Moreover, the supine values were
obtained from a single measurement rather than from two consecutive consistent
readings.

For each standing measurement, the changes with respect to the supine values were
computed as
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\mathrm{HR}_t = \mathrm{HR}_t - \mathrm{HR}_0, \qquad
  \Delta\mathrm{SBP}_t = \mathrm{SBP}_t - \mathrm{SBP}_0, \qquad
  \Delta\mathrm{DBP}_t = \mathrm{DBP}_t - \mathrm{DBP}_0,
\end{equation}
where the subscript $0$ denotes the supine measurement. Pulse pressure was defined as
$\mathrm{PP} = \mathrm{SBP} - \mathrm{DBP}$ and mean arterial pressure as
$\mathrm{MAP} = \mathrm{DBP} + (\mathrm{SBP} - \mathrm{DBP})/3$. Each test was then
summarised by the following daily indices: the maximum and the mean of $\Delta$HR,
the minimum and the mean of $\Delta$SBP, and the minimum and the mean of PP over the
available standing measurements.

Stroke volume (SV) was not measured directly. An estimate was obtained using the formula proposed 
by Liljestrand and Zander \citep{liljestrand1928}, which expresses SV as a function of Systolic 
and Diastolic blood pressure:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SV} = K\frac{SBP - DBP}{SBP + DBP}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is a constant to be determined empirically. In a longitudinals study, like the present, 
the constant $K$ has no effect on the relative changes of SV, which are the quantities of interest. 
Next, cardiacc output (CO) was estimated as the product of SV and HR, and cardiac power (CP) as 
the product of CO and  MAP:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CO} = \mathrm{SV} \cdot \mathrm{HR}, \qquad
  \mathrm{CP} = \mathrm{CO} \cdot \mathrm{MAP}
\end{equation}
It may be useful to remeber that SV represents the volume of blood pumped by the left ventricle in one beat, 
CO represents the volume of blood pumped by the left ventricle in one minute, and CP represents the mechanical
power generated by the left ventricle to pump blood through the systemic circulation. 

To describe the average response to standing over the whole study, each variable was
plotted as a function of the minute of standing, and a second-degree polynomial was
fitted to all the available measurements pooled together (Figure~\ref{fig:lean-hr}).

\subsubsection{Body composition}\label{sec:bia}

Body weight and body composition were measured with a bioelectrical impedance analysis
(BIA) scale (Healtheep FG263LB) connected via Bluetooth to a smartphone running the
Fitdays application. Measurements were performed in the morning, after voiding, with the subject
barefoot and wearing only light clothing. The scale uses a four-electrode, foot-to-foot
configuration, with two electrodes on the front and two on the back of each foot. The
scale measures the impedance of the body to a weak alternating current at 50 kHz, and
estimates the body compartments from the impedance by means of proprietary equations
that also include weight, height, sex, and age \citep{kyle2004}. The scale measures weight
 directly, while all the other quantities are derived from the impedance by the proprietary equations of
the manufacturer, which are not disclosed. Their absolute values should therefore be
interpreted with caution, and in this study they were used only to follow changes over
time in the same subject. Measurements were available from \biaStart{} to \biaEnd{},
for a total of \biaDays{} days. 

The following variables, exported by the application, were considered: weight, body
mass index (BMI), body fat, subcutaneous fat, body water, skeletal muscle, and protein
(as percentages of body weight); muscle mass and bone mass (in kg); the visceral fat
index; and the basal metabolic rate (BMR, in kcal/day). The masses of skeletal muscle,
subcutaneous fat, and body fat were obtained by multiplying the corresponding
percentages by the weight. 

Since the scale was not used every day, the series were completed by imputation. The
days between two measurements were filled by linear interpolation. 

\subsubsection{Electroencephalography}\label{sec:eeg}

Resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) was recorded with a consumer-grade wearable
headband (Muse S Athena, Interaxon), a type of device whose use for research has been
validated against laboratory systems \citep{krigolson2017}. The headband has four dry
electrodes, two frontal (AF7, AF8) and two temporo-parietal, behind the ears (TP9,
TP10), with the reference at Fpz. Recordings started on \eegStart{} and, up to \eegEnd{}, 
were available for \eegDays{} days, and were collected once a day, 
simultaneously with the EEG and HRV recordings, at rest. The measurements were performed in the same room, 
with the subject laying supine on a bed, in a quiet environment, with the lights 
off and the eyes closed, after at least five minutes of rest in the same position. 
 The raw signals of the four channels were acquired with the Mind Monitor application and exported in text format,
one file per recording \citep{clutterbuck_mindmonitor}. The same files also contain the
optical channels used for fNIRS (Section~\ref{sec:fnirs}). Only the first recording of
each day started before 17:00 was retained, and the first and last \SI{10}{\second} of
each recording were removed. The sampling frequency $F_s$ was estimated for each
recording from the number of samples and their time stamps.

Mind Monitor marks the samples at which it detects eye blinks and jaw clenches, the
main sources of artefacts in frontal and temporal recordings. For each of these
events, the samples of all four channels within \SI{0.5}{\second} of the event were
removed. Each recording was then divided into consecutive, non-overlapping windows of
\SI{10}{\second}, giving a frequency resolution of \SI{0.1}{\hertz}. Windows that
contained removed samples were discarded.

For each window and channel, the amplitude spectrum was computed by fast Fourier
transform with the R package eegkit \citep{helwig_eegkit}, and the power spectral
density was taken as the square of the amplitude. The power in each of the classical
bands, delta (\SIrange{0.5}{4}{\hertz}), theta (\SIrange{4}{8}{\hertz}), alpha
(\SIrange{8}{13}{\hertz}), beta (\SIrange{13}{30}{\hertz}), and gamma
(\SIrange{30}{45}{\hertz}), was obtained by integrating the spectrum over the band with
the trapezoidal rule. It was then divided by the total power between 0.5 and
\SI{45}{\hertz}, giving the relative power of the band:
\begin{equation}
  P^{\mathrm{rel}}_{c,b} = \frac{\int_{b} |X_c(f)|^2\,df}{\int_{0.5}^{45} |X_c(f)|^2\,df},
\end{equation}
where $X_c(f)$ is the amplitude spectrum of channel $c$ and $b$ is the band. Relative
powers were preferred to absolute powers because they are independent of the overall
amplitude of the signal, which, for dry electrodes, depends on the contact between the
electrodes and the skin and may change from one session to another. For each channel
and band, the relative powers were averaged over the windows of the recording, giving
one value per day.

Besides the 20 channel--band combinations, three indices were computed for each band by
summing the relative powers over channels: over all four channels (e.g.\
\texttt{Alpha}), over the left channels AF7 and TP9 (\texttt{Alpha\_L}), and over the
right channels AF8 and TP10 (\texttt{Alpha\_R}). 

\subsubsection{Functional near-infrared spectroscopy}\label{sec:fnirs}

Changes in the concentration of oxygenated (HbO$_2$) and deoxygenated (Hb) haemoglobin
in the frontal region were estimated by continuous-wave functional near-infrared
spectroscopy (fNIRS), using the optical sensors of a consumer-grade wearable headband
(Muse S Athena, Interaxon), the same device used for the EEG recordings. The theory
behind the method and its adaptation to this device are described in detail in a
previous work \citep{maccallini2026fnirs}, to which the reader is referred for the
derivation of the equations reported below. Recordings started on \fnirsStart{} and, 
up to \fnirsEnd{}, were available for \fnirsDays{} days, and were collected once a day, 
simultaneously with the EEG and HRV recordings, at rest. The measurements were performed in the same room, 
with the subject laying supine on a bed, in a quiet environment, with the lights 
off and the eyes closed, after at least five minutes of rest in the same position. 

The headband has a single optical source at the centre of the forehead, emitting at
\SIlist{660;730;850}{\nano\metre}, and two receivers on each side: an inner receiver at
about \SI{1}{\centi\metre} and an outer receiver at about \SI{3}{\centi\metre} from the
source. Each receiver also records ambient light, with the source switched off. The raw
intensities were acquired with the Mind Monitor application and exported in text
format, one file per recording, with the 16 optical channels assigned as in
Table~\ref{tab:optics} \citep{clutterbuck_mindmonitor}. Only the first recording of each
day started before 17:00 was retained, recordings without full optical data were discarded,
and the first and last \SI{10}{\second} of each recording were removed. Each channel
was then summarised by its mean intensity over the recording, giving one value per day
for each receiver and wavelength.

Haemoglobin concentrations were derived from the \SI{730}{\nano\metre} and
\SI{850}{\nano\metre} channels using the modified Beer--Lambert law
\citep{delpy1988}. Since the intensity emitted by the source is unknown, only changes
in attenuation with respect to a reference can be measured. As a reference, the mean
of the daily intensities over the whole study, $\bar{I}_{r,\lambda}$, was used for each
receiver $r$ and wavelength $\lambda$, under the assumption that all the recordings were
performed in the same resting conditions. The change in attenuation on day $d$ is then
\begin{equation}
  \Delta A_{r,\lambda}(d) = \ln\frac{\bar{I}_{r,\lambda}}{I_{r,\lambda}(d)} .
\end{equation}
The optical path was taken as $x_{r,\lambda} = L_r \cdot \mathrm{DPF}(\lambda, a)$, where
$L_r$ is the source--receiver distance and DPF is the differential pathlength factor of
the frontal human head, which depends on the wavelength $\lambda$ (in nm) and on the age
$a$ (in years) of the subject \citep{scholkmann2013}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{DPF}(\lambda, a) = 223.3 + 0.05624\,a^{0.8493} - 5.723\cdot10^{-7}\lambda^3
  + 0.001245\,\lambda^2 - 0.9025\,\lambda .
\end{equation}
The age was computed as the calendar year of the recording minus the year of birth.
This equation was derived for wavelengths between 690 and \SI{832}{\nano\metre}
\citep{scholkmann2013}, so its value at \SI{850}{\nano\metre} is a small extrapolation.
For each receiver and day, the changes in concentration were obtained by solving the
linear system
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \varepsilon_{\mathrm{Hb}}(\lambda_1) & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{HbO_2}}(\lambda_1) \\
    \varepsilon_{\mathrm{Hb}}(\lambda_2) & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{HbO_2}}(\lambda_2)
  \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} \Delta[\mathrm{Hb}] \\ \Delta[\mathrm{HbO_2}] \end{bmatrix}
  =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \Delta A_{r,\lambda_1} / x_{r,\lambda_1} \\
    \Delta A_{r,\lambda_2} / x_{r,\lambda_2}
  \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad \lambda_1 = \SI{730}{\nano\metre},\ \lambda_2 = \SI{850}{\nano\metre},
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Hb}}$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HbO_2}}$ are the molar
extinction coefficients tabulated by Prahl \citep{prahl_hb}: 1102.2 and 390 at
\SI{730}{\nano\metre}, and 691.32 and 1058 at \SI{850}{\nano\metre}, in
\si{\per\centi\metre\per\molar}. These values follow the decadic convention of optical
density and were multiplied by $\ln 10$ to be consistent with the natural logarithm used
for the attenuation. With paths in centimetres, concentrations are obtained in
\si{\molar}.

For each receiver, two derived indices were computed: the change in total haemoglobin,
$\Delta[\mathrm{HbTot}] = \Delta[\mathrm{HbO_2}] + \Delta[\mathrm{Hb}]$, a proxy for
local blood volume, and the difference
$\Delta[\mathrm{HbDiff}] = \Delta[\mathrm{HbO_2}] - \Delta[\mathrm{Hb}]$, an index of
oxygenation, whose positive values indicate that the increase in oxygenated blood is
greater than the increase in deoxygenated blood, with respect to the reference. Therefore,
$\Delta[\mathrm{HbDiff}]$ helps to dentify days on which the increase in concentration of
frontal oxygenated blood is not only due to an increase in blood volume.

Frontal indices were obtained by summing the values of the left and right receivers,
separately for the inner and the outer receivers. Because the light reaching the inner
receivers travels mostly through the scalp and skull, the outer channels are expected
to be the more sensitive to cortical haemodynamics. The \SI{660}{\nano\metre} and
ambient channels were not used for the concentrations, but their daily means were
retained as additional variables.

\subsection{Dynamic regression}\label{sec:dynareg}

Measurements repeated on the same subject over time are not independent, and
methods that assume independence, such as correlation coefficients, $t$-tests, and
standard regression, are therefore not suited to N-of-1 observational data, although
they have often been used in this context \citep{mcdonald2017}. In particular, in the
presence of positive autocorrelation, standard regression tends to overestimate the
significance of a predictor \citep{montgomery2012}. Among the methods proposed for
these data, dynamic regression is one of the simplest
\citep{vieira2017,mcdonald2020}: the outcome is regressed on the predictors and on
its own past values, introduced as endogenous covariates, which absorb the
autocorrelation of the series. An implementation in R of the pipeline described by
McDonald and colleagues \citep{mcdonald2020} was presented in a previous work
\citep{maccallini2024dynreg}. Here it is extended in three directions: predictors are
summarised over time windows of increasing length, to capture delayed and cumulative
effects; the number of lags is selected automatically and a time term is included to
account for long-term trends; and the significance of the results is corrected for
the large number of predictors and windows tested.

\paragraph{Preparation of covariates.}
For each covariate, the number of days of use and non-use (for non-negative
covariates, such as drug doses), the number of available days, and the coefficient of
variation were first computed on the raw data. Gaps of up to five consecutive days
were then filled by linear interpolation \citep{zoo}, and longer gaps were left
missing. Each covariate was detrended by taking the residuals of its linear regression
on time, and standardised to zero mean and unit variance. Interactions between
covariates were included as the product of the raw daily values of the variables
involved, computed before the steps above and then processed like any other
covariate. Whenever a product was included, the variables forming it were also
included as covariates.

\paragraph{Time windows.}
Since the effect of a covariate on symptoms may be delayed and may accumulate over
several days, each covariate $x$ was summarised over windows of increasing length
$n$. For $n=0$, the value on the same day as the outcome was used. For $n \geq 1$, the
covariate on day $t$ was replaced by its weighted mean over the $n$ preceding days,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{x}^{(n)}_t = \frac{\sum_{d=1}^{n} w_d\, x_{t-d}}{\sum_{d=1}^{n} w_d},
  \qquad w_d = \frac{M-d}{M-1},
\end{equation}
where the sums run over the days with available data and $M = 32$. The weights
decrease linearly with the distance from the outcome, from 1 on the previous day to 0
for the day $M$ days earlier, so the effect of a covariate is assumed to fade linearly
over about one month. The windows considered were $n = 0, 1, 8, 16, 32$ days
\citep{runner}. Each windowed covariate was standardised again, so its coefficients
are expressed per standard deviation.

\paragraph{Outcome.}
The outcome was standardised without imputation, so that days without a measurement
did not contribute to the models. Two versions were analysed: the standardised
outcome itself (linear regression), and a binary variable equal to 1 on days in the
upper quartile of the outcome and 0 otherwise (logistic regression).

\paragraph{Models.}
For each covariate, window, and version of the outcome, the following model was
fitted:
\begin{equation}
  g\left(\mathrm{E}[y_t]\right) = \beta_0 + \beta\, \tilde{x}^{(n)}_t + \gamma\, t
  + \sum_{l=1}^{p} \phi_l\, y_{t-l},
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the identity for the linear models and the logit for the logistic
models, $t$ is time in days, and $y_{t-l}$ is the standardised outcome $l$ days
earlier. The time term accounts for the long-term trend of the outcome, and the
lagged outcomes account for its autocorrelation, so $\beta$ measures the association
of the covariate with the outcome beyond what is expected from the trend and the
recent course of the outcome. The number of lags $p$ was selected with the Akaike
information criterion \citep{akaike1974}, among autoregressive models of order up to
30 fitted to the linearly detrended outcome.
% TODO: value of p used for each outcome (e.g. p = 14 for Score_m).
Days with missing values in any term of a model were excluded from that model.

The models were fitted in two ways. In the marginal analysis, each covariate entered
a separate model, so its coefficient reflects all the associations that the covariate
shares with other, correlated covariates. In this analysis, products of covariates
were treated as composite exposures. In the joint analysis, all the covariates of a
predefined group (e.g.\ meteorological variables) entered the same model, so each
coefficient is adjusted for the other covariates of the group. Interactions were
interpreted only in this analysis, where the product is estimated together with its
main effects.

\paragraph{Multiple comparisons.}
For each outcome, version of the outcome, type of analysis, and group of covariates,
the $p$-values of all covariates and windows were corrected jointly with the
Benjamini--Hochberg procedure \citep{benjamini1995}, and associations with an adjusted
$p < 0.05$ were considered significant, which corresponds to a false discovery rate of
5\%. For associations with an unadjusted $p < 0.05$, component-plus-residual plots
were produced \citep{fox_car}. For the joint analysis, the signed $-\log_{10}$ of the
adjusted $p$-values was plotted for each covariate as a function of the window length,
positive for positive coefficients and negative for negative ones.
